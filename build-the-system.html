Build the System, Then Let AI Play

Design systems are one of the most underrated tools in marketing right now, and they're the reason AI works for me instead of against me.

Lauren MitchellNovember 19, 2026
tokens.json

My calendar looks pretty similar every season. There's a holiday sale, a new flavor drop and usually a launch or a contest, and every one of them needs emails, site sections, ads and social posts by yesterday.

Coming up with ideas was never the hard part. What ate my time was rebuilding the same pieces for every campaign and second-guessing small details, like which blue we use or how chunky the button shadow should be.

So I stopped trying to work faster and started building a system instead.

What a design system actually is (for marketers)

If you come from product design, you probably think of a design system as a component library for an app. The marketing version is simpler and a little scrappier. It's one place that defines how a brand looks, sounds and behaves wherever it shows up.

Mine has four layers.

  • Tokens: the exact colors, type sizes and spacing, each with a name so nobody has to remember hex codes.
  • Components: the pieces I reuse constantly, like email headers, product cards, buttons and section layouts.
  • Voice rules: the words we use, the words we never use and how the brand sounds when it's excited versus when it's apologizing.
  • Real examples: past emails, pages and ads that show the rules in action.

Why it matters more in marketing than people think

Marketing teams ship more often and across more channels than almost anyone else in a company. Without a system, each campaign drifts a little further from the last one, and customers pick up on it even if they couldn't explain what feels off.

Having one changes a few things.

  • Speed. Every project starts halfway done instead of on a blank page.
  • Consistency. Emails, pages and ads feel like the same brand even when different people make them.
  • Room to play. With the basics locked in, I get to put my energy into the concept and the story. That's how you end up with an arcade-themed Black Friday.
"The system handles the rules, so you get to spend your time on the ideas."

Your design system is also your best AI prompt

I didn't expect this part. Once the brands I work on had real design systems, the AI tools I use got a lot more useful.

AI only knows what you tell it. If you ask for "a holiday email," you'll get something generic that could belong to anyone. When I give it our tokens, components, voice rules and a few past emails, the first draft comes back looking and sounding like us. It still needs edits, but it's on brand and much closer to done.

The design system used to be a document nobody opened. Now it's the instructions every tool works from, AI included.

Use AI as a super tool, not a replacement

A lot of people are worried that AI is coming for creative jobs. I think it's much more likely to leave behind the people who ignore it than the ones who learn to drive it.

The way I see it, I'm the creative director and AI is a really fast production crew. It can rough out a Shopify section or write ten subject lines in a few minutes. It can't decide what's on brand, what's actually funny or what our customers care about this season. That judgment is still the job, and it's still ours.

"The people who learn to drive AI are the ones it works for."

How I stay up to date (without losing my mind)

AI tools change almost every week, and keeping up can feel like a lot. These habits help.

  1. Try one new thing a week. It can be a feature, a tool or a different way of prompting. Small experiments add up.
  2. Test it on real work. The best way to learn a tool is to point it at an actual campaign, not a toy example.
  3. Keep what works and drop the rest. Not every shiny tool deserves a spot in your workflow.
  4. Write down your wins. When a workflow saves you hours, turn it into a process you and your tools can repeat.
  5. Share it with your team. One person's shortcut becomes everyone's head start.

Build your own: a starter checklist

If you want to build one for your brand, here's where I'd start.

  1. Gather everything. Pull together your brand guidelines, packaging, best emails and top social posts.
  2. Pull out the tokens. Give every color, font, size and button style a name.
  3. Write the voice rules. A short list of do's and don'ts, plus the words you never say.
  4. Build five core components. Start with an email header and footer, a product card, a button and one hero section.
  5. Test it. Ask AI to make a new campaign using only your system, then fix what it gets wrong and update the rules.
  6. Keep it alive. Every new campaign either uses the system or makes it better.

The tools are going to keep changing. I think the brands that do well will be the ones that know exactly who they are and can hand that to any tool or teammate quickly.

Build the system. Then let AI play.
